Website Accessibility for Small Businesses: A Practical Guide

An accessible website is easier for more people to understand, navigate, and use—and that usually makes it a better website for everyone.

Accessibility means removing avoidable barriers. Someone may use a keyboard instead of a mouse, enlarge text, rely on captions, browse with a screen reader, or need stronger colour contrast. A thoughtful website gives those visitors a clear way to understand your offer and take action.

Start with readable content

Use clear language, useful headings, comfortable line height, and sufficient contrast. Short paragraphs and descriptive subheadings help people scan, especially on mobile or when they are reading under pressure.

Make navigation work without a mouse

Every interactive element should be reachable with a keyboard and have a visible focus state. Menus, buttons, forms, accordions, and links should follow a logical order so someone can move through the page without becoming lost.

Accessibility habit

Try using your website with the mouse disconnected. If you cannot reach a key action or understand where you are, a visitor may face the same barrier.

Write useful image descriptions

Meaningful images need concise alt text that explains their purpose. Decorative images can be ignored by assistive technology. Avoid stuffing keywords into alt text; describe what a visitor needs to know.

Build better forms and buttons

Labels should explain what each field requires, error messages should identify how to fix a problem, and buttons should describe the action they perform. “Send enquiry” is more useful than a vague button that says “Submit.”

Accessibility supports business growth

Accessible design improves clarity, mobile usability, search understanding, and customer confidence. It can also help you serve people you might otherwise lose because the website was difficult to use. Small improvements across the whole journey can make a meaningful difference.
